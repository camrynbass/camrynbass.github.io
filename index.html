Lung cancer and small cell lung carcinoma

Despite modern decreasing rates of cigarette smoking, lung cancers remain the most deadly and common type of cancer in Canada. (1)

Carcinoma-type lung cancer is split into two major categories: small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) and non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC). SCLC represents approximately 12% of lung cancers diagnosed each year globally, (2) with most cases occuring in cigarette smokers. (3) SCLC patients have a worse prognosis compared to NSCLC patients. (4)

Table data from Moore, S. M. et al. The Canadian Small Cell Lung Cancer Database (CASCADE): Results from a Multi-Institutional Real-World Evidence Collaboration. Journal of Thoracic Oncology 17, S244:EP03.01-016 (2022).

Unfortunately, most SCLC patients do not respond to therapeutics in the long-term. There is a critical need to understand the biological factors driving the varied responses to chemo- and immunotherapeutics, and hopefully improve outcomes.

SCLC is characterized by solid histopathological morphology, granular cell nuclei (also known as "salt-and-pepper" nuclei), high nucleus:cytosol ratio, high mitotic rate, and inactivating muations in the tumor-suppressor genes TP53 and RB1 (5)

Recent research has attempted to develop systems to classify subtypes of SCLC that are can predict clinical outcomes and improve therapeutic targeting. One proposed method of classifying SCLC tumors is based on dominant gene expression of neuroendocrine (NE) biomarkers ASCL1 and NEUROD1, and non-NE biomarkers POU2F3 and YAP1/Inflamed.

However, these biomarkers are not prognostic, which limits their clinical utility. My master's research project aims to address this gap by uncovering novel prognostic traits that may serve clinical utility and provide targets for future therapeutic development.
